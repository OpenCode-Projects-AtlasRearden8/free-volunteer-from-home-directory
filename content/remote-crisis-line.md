# Remote Crisis-Line Volunteering

Sit with someone during one of the worst moments of their life — from your
kitchen table. Modern crisis lines increasingly work by **text and chat**:
volunteers type with people experiencing anxiety, grief, loneliness, self-harm
thoughts, or mental-health crises, using structured, well-trained
conversational techniques.

Two well-known examples in the United States are **Crisis Text Line** and
**988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** (which uses chat/text alongside its phone
line and trains volunteers through partner crisis centers). Similar text-based
services exist in other countries — search for "text crisis line volunteer"
plus your country.

## Who this suits

Calm, patient listeners. Good typists. People who can hold someone else's
pain without needing to fix it. Not recommended right now if you are yourself
in a fragile season — the training is thorough, but honest self-assessment
matters.

## Time commitment

- This is the **highest-commitment item in this directory** — please read
  carefully before signing up.
- Expect roughly **30 hours of training** (online, self-paced with live
  components, over several weeks).
- After training, expect roughly **4–5 hours per week**, usually as
  scheduled 2-hour shifts, plus a minimum-hours-per-month commitment for
  most programs.
- Shifts can often be taken overnight or early morning, which suits
  night-owls.

## Age notes

- **18+ for almost all crisis-line volunteer roles.** Some programs require
  you to be 18 or older and out of high school.
- A background check (often with a fee, sometimes reimbursable) is standard
  because you'd be supporting vulnerable people.
- If you're a teen who wants to help: consider peer-listening training
  through your school, or crisis-line work is a great goal for adulthood —
  many volunteers started as teens who wished they could help.

## Signing-up steps

1. Choose a program: in the U.S., **Crisis Text Line** and **988** (via
   partner crisis centers) both run volunteer text/chat roles; outside the
   U.S., search for your national helpline's volunteer page.
2. Complete the online application (about 20–30 minutes; asks about
   availability, motivation, and experience).
3. Complete any interview or reference step the program requires.
4. Pass the background check (see the age notes).
5. Complete the training course — role-play conversations with a coach, live
   components, quizzes, and a final certification.
6. Sign up for shifts on the program's volunteer scheduler.
7. Start. Your first shifts are usually supervised or closely reviewed, and
   support staff are always one click away during a conversation.

## A note on the emotional load

Volunteering here is genuinely meaningful — and genuinely heavy sometimes.
Good programs bake in supervision, debriefs, and the ability to end a
conversation and step away. Before you commit:

- Talk it through with someone who knows you.
- Start with the lower-commitment entries in this directory if you're unsure;
  crisis-line work will still be here in six months.
- Once you begin, use the program's self-care and debrief resources — they
  exist because volunteers need them.
