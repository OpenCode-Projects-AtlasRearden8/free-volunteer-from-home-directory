# Craft Drives

Knit, crochet, sew, or assemble — then mail it. Blanket drives for children
in hospitals, hats and nests for wildlife rescues, lap blankets for veterans,
birthday kits for food pantries, socks and sweaters for shelter animals.
This is the oldest "kitchen table volunteering" of all, and mailing networks
make it fully remote: you create at home, post it to the coordinator, and
never attend a single meeting.

## Who this suits

People who like working with their hands; anyone who wants to volunteer
while watching TV; knitters with too much yarn (and friends and family who
want to hand theirs over "before the house fills up").

## Time commitment

- **Minimum:** one item. One hat. One blanket square. Many drives are
  modular — you make squares and someone else joins them.
- **Typical volunteer:** a few hours a week, in front of a show.
- Watch your own pace: repetitive strain is real. Ten minutes of hand
  stretching per hour is better than a week off with a sore wrist.

## Age notes

- **All ages welcome**, with adult supervision for scissors and needles
  based on the child's skill.
- The most common volunteer craft profile in these drives is
  **"experienced hobbyist," 16 to 80+.**
- Teens can earn service hours for documented craft donations in many
  school programs — save your mailing receipts to show them.

## Signing-up steps

1. Pick your cause and your craft level:
   - **Project Linus** — handmade blankets for children in crisis or in
     hospitals (knit, crochet, sew, or fleece-tie); local chapters
     coordinate drop-off or mailing.
   - **Knit-a-Square** — blankets (and hats) assembled from 8-inch squares
     mailed in to help children in South Africa; you only need to make one
     square.
   - **Wildlife rescues** — many accept knitted nests and pouches for
     orphaned animals; rescue organizations list brief patterns on their
     sites.
     - **Your local hospital or NICU** — some accept tiny hats and blankets;
     call or email first, because accepted sizes and fabric rules matter a
     lot and every ward has its own guidelines.
2. Email or check the website of the organization you chose and confirm:
   accepted items, preferred patterns/sizes, mailing address, and whether a
   contact form or donations form is required for shipping.
3. Make your item(s) to their pattern and size requirements.
4. Wash, dry, and label items per their instructions (most ask for a
   no-scent detergent and no adornments like buttons or googly eyes).
5. Mail, or drop off if a local chapter exists. Include a simple note with
   your name so they can acknowledge receipt — and so you get proof for
   school service hours if you need it.
6. Repeat next month. Drives love repeat volunteers more than one-time
   marathon knitters — small steady production keeps their shelves stocked.

## Tips

- Choose the drive *before* buying yarn; most have size and material rules.
- Squares and hats are the beginner's best friend: you learn to tension and
  consistent sizing on small, quick items rather than committing a month to
  one project.
- No yarn budget? Post that you're looking for spare yarn in a local
  "free" or swap group and check thrift stores — half of craft-volunteer
  yarn is donated from other people's stashes.
