Accessibility Testing

Help make apps, websites, and devices usable for people with disabilities — screen-reader users, people with limited motor control, people with color blindness or low vision. Disabled testers who use assistive technology every day are paid for structured user testing on platforms; and everyone, disabled or not, can contribute to accessibility as a volunteer through documentation, beta testing, and community QA programs.

Who this suits

  • If you use assistive tech (screen reader, switch access, magnification, voice control): platforms and product teams genuinely want your paid participation in user studies — this is often work, not volunteering.
  • If you don't: you can still volunteer as an extra set of eyes. Open-source apps, nonprofit projects, and community programs welcome volunteers who test their sites with a keyboard only, check color contrast, and review whether alternative text is missing on images.

Time commitment

  • Minimum session: 30 minutes — e.g. keyboard-only walk through your favorite library's website and note what breaks.
  • Typical volunteer: 1–2 hours a month is a real contribution.
  • Volunteering here is "small doses, often" rather than one big session.

Age notes

  • Volunteer testing roles through communities or open-source projects: usually 16+, sometimes 13+ with parent permission — check the program.
  • Paid evaluator platforms are typically 18+.
  • No technical degree required. Curiosity and a willingness to follow a checklist are enough to start.

Signing-up steps

  1. Learn three free checks anyone can do (this is your training, and it is under an hour total):
    • Tab test: unplug your mouse. Can you reach and operate everything with Tab / Enter / Space? Can you always see where focus is?
    • Contrast check: use a free contrast checker on the site's text colors. Low-contrast gray-on-white text is the most common failure.
    • Missing alt text: on any image-heavy page, note which images have no meaningful description (right-click an image in most browsers and choose "Inspect" to see the alt attribute).
  2. Find a target: pick a nonprofit you care about, an open-source project you use, or a local government website — these often have thin accessibility budgets.
  3. Write up what you found in plain sentences, with the URL and what you did — you do not need WCAG paragraph numbers to be useful.
  4. Deliver it: email their contact address, or post in the project's GitHub issues or community forum. ("Hi, I tried your site with keyboard only and hit these two problems…" is a welcome message almost everywhere.)
  5. If you want deeper involvement: join the accessibility channel of an open-source project, or a community program, and work through issues alongside the maintainers.

Why this entry matters so much

One finding from a volunteer can save thousands of users a frustrating experience — and unlike most volunteering, there is a real chance your result changes a live product within weeks.
